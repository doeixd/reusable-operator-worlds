## I like the over-alignment hypothesis, with one caveat

The pattern is very consistent with it: prospective pressure -> make
related tasks look alike -> erase member-specific coordinates ->
few-shot identification gets harder. The support-size curve is
especially suggestive: harm largest at k=1, diminishing as more evidence
arrives. That's exactly what you'd expect if the learner made the family
mean easier to represent but made the residual identity of individual
family members harder to infer.

But I would call this the LEADING mechanism, not yet the mechanism.
There's a closely related alternative: prospective training worsened the
CONDITIONING of the adaptation problem without literally collapsing
representations toward a mean. Those can look identical in Phi.

So while H35 runs, add a cheap mechanistic audit. For each family
member, measure d_between = E_{i!=j}||z_i - z_j||^2 against within-member
variation d_within, and define a discrimination ratio D =
d_between / d_within — or the functional equivalent rather than latent
coordinates. If D_prospective < D_ordinary, especially on the dimensions
the task adaptor uses, then over-alignment becomes much more than a
story.

An even better measure would be the Fisher/Jacobian sensitivity of query
behavior to the new task code. If prospective training reduces
||df/dc_task||, then the representation has literally become less
responsive to the few-shot variable that distinguishes family members.
That would explain the result beautifully.

# H32 is actually a very important negative lesson

R_effective 0.762 -> 0.791 while Phi 0 -> -8.58. So more recoverable
shared family geometry did NOT produce a more useful representation.
That's a major methodological result:

    Representations can become "more structured" by our geometric
    measure while becoming worse languages for learning.

This is exactly why making Phi primary was right, and I would elevate it
in the V6 closure. V5 taught geometry != economics. V6 now adds
geometry != fertility. Even the right-looking structure can be
destructive if it collapses distinctions required for rapid
specialization. So the real target isn't "maximize shared structure" —
it is SEPARATE WHAT SHOULD BE SHARED FROM WHAT MUST REMAIN CHEAPLY
IDENTIFIABLE.

# This points toward a richer representation model

Perhaps the right family representation isn't T_i ~ S but
T_i = S(alpha_i) + eps_i. We've already encountered this exact form
economically; now it may matter dynamically too. A fertile
representation needs SHARED COORDINATES that cheaply encode "what family
am I in / what computation recurs?" and simultaneously ARGUMENT
COORDINATES that make alpha_i very cheap to infer from a few examples.
The prospective objective you implemented may be rewarding the first
while accidentally crushing the second.

So think of fertility as two factors: shared prior quality (family
compression) plus argument identifiability (fast specialization). This
is closely related to the old typed-code distinction REFERENCE /
ARGUMENT / INNOVATION. That lesson may be coming back at a deeper level.
The learner should not be told "make related tasks identical"; it should
learn "make the shared part identical while giving differences a
low-dimensional, easy-to-infer coordinate system."

# A candidate V6 successor objective

Rather than min_R L_query(Adapt(R, D_sibling)), which can apparently
reduce the incentive to preserve distinguishing information, the right
target eventually becomes something like

    min  L_query + lambda D*(R) + beta C_infer(alpha_new)

where adaptation is constrained to a known ARGUMENT CHANNEL: S_theta is
slow and shared, and new tasks may only learn alpha plus a small
innovation eps. Then the prospective objective explicitly asks: is my
shared representation such that a new family member can be acquired by
learning only a small argument? That is much closer to what H20 schema
economics says should be valuable.

But do NOT implement this yet. First let H35 and allocation finish. This
is the likely V7/V6R mechanism if the current negative survives.

# A possibility worth keeping in mind: the objective may point the wrong way

Meta-learning intuition says optimize the representation so a few
adaptation steps work well. But if those steps operate only over
task-local codes while shared weights receive the outer gradient, the
cheapest outer solution can be to MOVE THE SHARED PREDICTION TOWARD THE
EXPECTED MEMBER rather than to build coordinates in which member
identity is quickly inferable. The outer objective cares about the
endpoint after adaptation, not why adaptation succeeded. It can improve
that endpoint either by making adaptation effective, or by making
adaptation unnecessary. Your intervention seems to have drifted toward
the second.

And then, paradoxically, unseen members with small support become harder
because the shared prediction has become a strong prior around the wrong
family-average point. That would explain why the corrected prospective
and explicit-sharing objectives have similar qualitative effects even
though they are no longer implementation-identical. If so, the fix isn't
a better optimizer: we need to distinguish reusable prior from rapidly
identifiable arguments in the architecture and the objective.

# This may alter the grand theory in a useful way

We've often phrased the goal as "make future learning cheaper." V6 shows
that MAKING A FUTURE TASK CLOSER TO WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW IS NOT
EQUIVALENT TO MAKING IT EASY TO LEARN. A representation can have a very
strong prior and still be awful for updating. So a good representation
needs compression of regularity AND identifiability of novelty. This is
almost Bayesian: the shared representation is a prior, while alpha_task
and eps_task must form a likelihood-sensitive coordinate system. Too
strong a prior crushes the likelihood.

# One methodological point I would preserve prominently

The sequence in this run is worth documenting because it demonstrates
why the guards matter: a frozen basis made all interventions secretly
identical; the supervised objective had zero shared gradient; apparent
future tasks had already been trained on; a supposedly adaptive probe
did not adapt; the prospective inner loop itself later turned out not to
adapt; and a weak positive world-0 result vanished across three worlds.
Any one of those could have produced a coherent but false scientific
story.

Extract a general NESTED-LEARNING AUDIT checklist:

    1. Does the intervention change parameters?
    2. Does the inner learner actually learn?
    3. Does the probe have dynamic range?
    4. Is the future genuinely unseen?
    5. Does the outer objective depend on inner adaptation?
    6. Do results replicate across worlds?

That's legitimately valuable research practice.

So let the two registered sweeps finish and build nothing else yet. If
H35 stays negative and allocation produces no fertile schema regime,
close V6 with a strong coherent negative: naively optimizing
representations for post-adaptation performance does not produce
fertility; it slightly increases shared family geometry while destroying
the member-specific information needed for rapid future adaptation. And
the next hypothesis becomes much more specific: fertile representation
requires factorizing reusable computation from cheaply inferable task
arguments, rather than merely pulling related tasks into a shared
representation. That is a substantially better hypothesis than the one
V6 started with.
