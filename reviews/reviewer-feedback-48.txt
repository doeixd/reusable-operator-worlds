This is a very good place to end V5. More importantly, I think it changes what the
next bottleneck is.

V5 did NOT end with "we need a better economic rule." The economics are now the
part that looks unusually clean.

The bottleneck has moved to:

    Can the learner actually construct representations in which the available
    economics can be exploited?

That is a much more interesting problem.

### What I think V5 has actually established

The strongest result is the recurrence of the same prospective amortization
relation at two different representational levels. For individual abstractions
H* ~= lambda D*(A) / s_bar_A, and sealed C1 gives chi = H* s_bar_A / (lambda
D*(A)) ~= 1 with mean absolute deviation only 0.016. Then one level higher, for
schemas, M* ~= D*(S) / s_bar_schema, with the threshold moving monotonically
with relatedness and the integer/ceiling prediction landing correctly across the
sealed worlds.

Even with C2's strict 15% clause unresolved, the important structural fact seems
to survive: fixed representation cost vs repeated prospective savings predicts
when another level of abstraction becomes economically justified. That is
exactly the recursive story we hoped might exist. It is now much more than
N s > C being a nice metaphor.

## But C3 may be the most important V5 result

You deliberately created worlds in which a higher-order factorization
economically EXISTS. The teacher operators have the structure. The
matched-budget oracle says there is something worth exploiting. Yet after
PROMOTE, FACTORIZE wins 0/6 at both meta-recurrence levels. And sealed makes the
discrepancy worse: unexplained fraction 0.921 versus 0.873 in development.
Meanwhile M > F in every cell.

That is telling us something very specific:

    PROMOTE can create useful abstractions without creating the right
    abstractions for recursive abstraction.

That's a major distinction. V3 showed recurrent computation can become a new
reusable object. V5 now says those objects do not automatically organize
themselves into a representation whose higher-order regularities remain
accessible. That is probably the next scientific bottleneck.

# I think there are now three notions of "good abstraction"

We've been implicitly conflating them.

1. LOCALLY USEFUL. Does A help tasks? s_bar_A > 0. V3 says yes.
2. ECONOMICALLY WORTHWHILE. Does its future reuse repay its description cost?
   N s_bar_A > D*(A). V5 says we can predict this remarkably well.
3. STRUCTURALLY FERTILE. Does creating A preserve/expose relationships that
   allow further abstraction? This is new.

A representation could be very useful and economical while being a terrible
substrate for A_1, A_2, ... -> S(alpha). And C3 suggests current PROMOTE may
produce exactly that. I would introduce this concept explicitly: ABSTRACTION
FERTILITY, or refactorability. The grand architecture doesn't merely need
abstractions that compress the present. It needs abstractions that remain
composable and abstractable in the future. That's a stronger requirement.

# Why might PROMOTE destroy the higher-order structure?

## Hypothesis A - fragmentation

The strongest clue is M > F, 12/12. Maybe one true family-level computation is
being represented by several promoted pieces: instead of A_f the learner creates
A_f1, A_f2, A_f3. Each is locally useful, but the clean teacher geometry becomes
fragmented across them. Then fitting S(alpha) over those promoted objects
naturally fails. This would make PROMOTE analogous to an oversegmentation
algorithm.

TEST. For each teacher family f, measure whether its functional contribution is
distributed across multiple promoted abstractions. Not merely "which abstraction
matches A_f best?" but min over subsets S of the library with |S| = k of
|A_f - Compose/Aggregate(S)|. If k=1 is poor but k=2,3 recovers the teacher
operator well, you have fragmentation. That would immediately explain M > F.

## Hypothesis B - the learner chooses a different, equally economical basis

Suppose teachers are A_1..A_4 but the learner discovers B_1..B_5 such that task
programs are cheap under the B's even though individual B_i do not correspond to
individual A_f. Then C3 isn't necessarily representational failure - it might be
a different basis with the same or better task economy. The teacher schema would
look absent only because we're asking whether the learner recreated the
teacher's decomposition. The proper test is global: D*(L_learned) +
D*(task programs | L_learned) versus the teacher-aligned representation. If the
learner's weird fragmented basis is equally cheap overall, we shouldn't force it
toward the teacher ontology. But because FACTORIZE loses on its own learned
library, there may still be a cost in future extensibility/search even if
current task description is fine.

## Hypothesis C - promotion destroys canonical coordinates

Even if two promoted objects implement related computation, their internal
functional coordinates might differ enough that the simple schema model cannot
see it. That connects directly to H28: f_i = d_i . A_i . e_i. Perhaps teacher
abstractions share A_i = S(alpha_i) but after learning we observe
A~_i = d_i . S(alpha_i) . e_i. Then direct factorization fails while
adapter-mediated factorization succeeds. If so, C3 is telling us that neural
abstraction requires quotienting out representational coordinates before
higher-order recurrence becomes visible. That is a very plausible bridge toward
real deep networks, where the same computation absolutely will not occur in
identical coordinates everywhere. It makes H28 look increasingly important.

## Hypothesis D - PROMOTE's objective is too myopic

V3 PROMOTE asks roughly "does this abstraction replace enough task-local
computation now?" It does not ask "will representing the computation this way
make future abstractions easier?" So perhaps it rationally finds locally
economical objects that are globally poor building blocks. The true objective
should eventually include current savings plus future representational
optionality: J(A) = D*(A) - N s_bar_A + eta E[C_future_abstraction | A]. I'm not
suggesting adding that term now. But conceptually this could be the origin of
the distinction between ordinary compression and representation learning for
compositional intelligence. A good primitive is not only one that compresses
what you've already seen; it is one that makes the next layer of explanation
easier to discover.

# C4 is useful precisely because it killed an attractive story

I would treat H27's original mechanism as dead. You had a tempting aggregate
observation D*_shared < D*_private and a plausible explanation. But the sealed D*
gap is only 6/12, the mean difference is tiny, spectral decay goes the wrong way
(0/12 sealed and 0/16 development), and the correlation flips dramatically
between development and sealed. So: do not build theory on "shared abstractions
are intrinsically more compressible per scalar." At least not yet. First
establish whether a stable per-world phenomenon exists at all. The pooling
problem matters. If H29 is designed to provide the missing matched snapshot, I'd
make that the next measurement.

# My priority from here

I would NOT go directly into H22-H24. The central scientific mystery has
changed. The economy already knows a schema should exist; the learner fails to
manufacture the objects over which the schema pays. That's much closer to the
ultimate goal than edit-selection accuracy.

1. H29 - LOCATE WHERE THE STRUCTURE DISAPPEARS. Use the fresh P_0 snapshots to
   determine whether meta-structure exists before PROMOTE and disappears after.
   The ideal diagnostic is R_meta(P_0) vs R_meta(L_promoted). If
   R_meta(P_0) >> R_meta(L), promotion is causally destroying an available
   family structure. If both are bad, the wake learner never represented the
   teacher meta-structure in a recoverable form. Very different research
   directions.

2. EXPLICITLY AUDIT FRAGMENTATION. Because M > F is so unanimous, do this almost
   regardless of H29. Measure teacher family -> number of promoted abstractions
   needed to reconstruct it, and the reverse, promoted abstraction -> number of
   teacher families contributing. This produces a functional mixing matrix. You
   may discover one teacher concept split across two learner concepts. That's
   actionable.

3. TRY A GLOBAL CONSOLIDATION ORACLE BEFORE CHANGING PROMOTE. Take the learned
   library as given and ask whether a different set of abstractions explains the
   same task behavior more cheaply and restores schema structure: L -> L' with
   recombination, rotation of the library basis, merge+split, adapter-mediated
   alignment. If an oracle can transform the ugly promoted library into a cheap
   factorable one without hurting tasks, you've established that local PROMOTE
   produces a representation that global sleep can improve. That would
   beautifully revive the original wake/sleep vision. Wake: solve locally.
   Sleep: refactor globally. V4 tried simple lifecycle edits and found no
   opportunity; V5 may have finally created the case where global
   representational refactoring actually has something substantive to do.

# This may be the bridge to V6

Originally V6 was going to be "make abstractions compositional." I would now add
a prerequisite: can the learner produce/refactor abstractions into a canonical
enough form that higher-order structure survives? A compositional language whose
words are arbitrary fragmented local solutions is going to be awful for program
synthesis. The goal isn't merely that A . B . C executes; we need A, B, C to be
GOOD WORDS. C3 shows "useful/promotable" and "good word" are not equivalent.
That's a major discovery.

# The ultimate project story has therefore become richer

    experience -> private solutions -> PROMOTE -> reusable computations
    -> REFACTOR / CANONICALIZE -> fertile computational vocabulary
    -> FACTORIZE / MACRO -> hierarchical language
    -> PROGRAM SYNTHESIS -> novel solutions

We had been missing the bolded step. V5 may have discovered why it is necessary.

# And this connects directly to "different ways of looking at things"

A representation can solve all current tasks while still being a bad way of
looking at the domain, because it makes the deeper regularity obscure. Another
representation can encode identical behavior but reveal A_i = S(alpha_i). The
second is preferable not merely because it currently saves more bits; it gives
the learner a better vocabulary for future abstraction, future composition,
extrapolating new family members, and program synthesis.

    A good abstraction doesn't merely summarize experience. It exposes structure
    that makes future thought cheaper.

C3 is our first empirical evidence that this distinction matters.

## So my reading of V5 is

DEMONSTRATED STRONGLY: prospective abstraction economics obey a surprisingly
precise amortization law, apparently recursively across atoms and schemas.

DEMONSTRATED NEGATIVELY: a learner that can create useful abstractions need not
create abstractions that expose higher-order reusable structure, even when such
structure is economically available.

FALSIFIED: the current "shared abstractions are intrinsically cheaper because
they have lower-dimensional coding geometry" story.

NEWLY EXPOSED BOTTLENECK: representation discovery/refactoring between
abstraction levels.

Not "how do we add another operator?" But: why does the economically correct
higher-order representation exist in the world yet fail to exist in the
learner's vocabulary - and can sleep discover the change of representation that
makes it visible? If we can solve that, the jump to V6/V7 becomes much more
credible, because we'd be moving toward a system that doesn't merely accumulate
primitives - it learns how to organize its primitives into an increasingly
powerful language.
