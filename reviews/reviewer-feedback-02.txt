## I also think the discrete result is more interesting now

The hard discrete model learned a remarkably accurate symbolic-ish representation:

* 92.2% exact explained routes;
* 96.4% route-position accuracy;
* primitive distance (0.00229);
* only ~768 inference multiply-adds;
* only 26,112 retained proxy bits.

Yet it pays a huge **online inference tax**. 

This actually maps nicely onto our discussion of loops/programs.

It tells us that separating the system into:

[
\boxed{\text{program representation}}
]

and

[
\boxed{\text{program inference}}
]

may eventually be critical.

The discrete library seems to be learning a real reusable “language.” It just doesn't yet have a good **compiler from demonstrations into that language**.

Continuous routing acts almost like a relaxed compiler: it can quickly move around a smooth operation space rather than having to determine an exact symbolic route.

That's possibly why it's winning online.

Long-term, I could imagine something like:

[
\text{continuous fast inference}
\rightarrow
\text{discrete consolidation}
]

during sleep.

That is:

**wake:** maintain uncertain/continuous mixtures because they're easy to optimize;

**sleep:** once enough evidence accumulates, compile them into cheap discrete calls/macros/loops.

That might be a better reason for wake/sleep than our earlier vague “compress periodically” argument.

## A new thought from the tuning results

The task-code LR being high for **both** winning models is telling us something.

Dense-C's best stage-one setting also uses:

[
\eta_{\text{task}}=0.05,
]

not just Continuous. 

So the core lifetime problem has a natural **two-timescale structure**:

[
\text{fast task inference}
]

and

[
\text{slow shared representation learning}.
]

That's exactly what we'd expect theoretically.

For each new task:

[
c_\tau
]

should move quickly as we infer “what task is this?”

Whereas:

[
\theta_{\text{shared}}
]

should accumulate more slowly across the lifetime.

That might eventually become architectural rather than just different optimizer LRs.

You could have:

[
c_{t+1}
=======

F(c_t,x_t,y_t)
]

as a fast learned inference process, while:

[
\theta_{t+1}
============

\theta_t-\eta_\theta\nabla L
]

changes slowly.

And then sleep/consolidation alters (\theta) or the library.

That's starting to look much more like a real continual-learning architecture.

## I wouldn't touch loops yet

Our loop discussion is interesting, but the agent should **not implement it now**.

The current experiment has a very specific job:

> Establish the economics of reusable neural computation.

Then the natural sequence becomes:

[
\text{fixed reuse}
\rightarrow
\text{graded reuse}
\rightarrow
\text{learned vocabulary size/structure}
\rightarrow
\text{variable-length calls}
\rightarrow
\text{loops/macros}.
]

Otherwise we'd mix the question:

> “Does reuse help?”

with the much harder question:

> “Can neural systems induce control flow?”

the learner discovers or changes the computational language itself.
	​


For example, start overcomplete and let it discover the right number/rank/granularity of reusable operations under a lifetime code-length objective; or let continuous fast representations consolidate into discrete reusable programs; or let repeated calls get compressed into macros/loops; or let the library fork/merge as the statistics of the world change.

That would move us beyond the very crowded literature on architectures designed to reuse computation toward your original question:

Can a neural system discover, and progressively improve, the reusable language in which its own computation is represented?
