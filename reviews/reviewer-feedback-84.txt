Yes. I think this gives us a **very concrete next architectural hypothesis**, and it targets exactly the failure E6 exposed.

The useful idea is not “use Tensor Product Representations because they are symbolic.” It is:

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{factor what computation is being performed from the role/context in which it is being used}
}
$$

That is almost perfectly aligned with the problem we just discovered.

Your uploaded note describes McCoy et al.'s central result as neural representations being approximately decomposable into reusable **fillers** bound to reusable **roles**, with systematic recombination even for combinations not seen during training.

Our current ROW representation is much cruder:

$$
p=(z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_D)
$$

where each $z_i$ is basically an opaque symbol.

E6 tells us that this works extremely well for execution, but at greater depth the symbol sequence ceases to be a stable local description of the computation. The same teacher motif may be realized as different learner trigrams, and eventually routes collapse toward attractor symbols.

So I think the next hypothesis should be:

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{the representation is entangling computational identity with structural role}
}
$$

and role–filler factorization might disentangle them.

---

# The direct analogy

McCoy-style:

$$
\text{DOG}\otimes\text{SUBJECT}
$$

means approximately:

> DOG, used in the SUBJECT role.

For us, something like:

$$
\boxed{
P_i\otimes R_j
}
$$

could mean:

> primitive $P_i$, used in structural role $R_j$.

Where roles might initially be:

$$
R_{\rm first},
R_{\rm middle},
R_{\rm last}
$$

but eventually much richer:

$$
R_{\rm predicate},
R_{\rm true-branch},
R_{\rm false-branch},
R_{\rm loop-body},
R_{\rm argument-1},
R_{\rm continuation},
\dots
$$

Then a program isn't merely:

$$
(z_1,z_2,z_3)
$$

but something closer to:

$$
\boxed{
P_a\otimes R_1
+
P_b\otimes R_2
+
P_c\otimes R_3.
}
$$

That is basically a parameterized combinator language realized geometrically.

---

# Why this could solve the E6 semantic problem

E6 observed:

$$
\text{same teacher computation}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{same learner substring}.
$$

There are at least two possible explanations.

### Bad explanation

The learner simply isn't representing reusable local computation anymore.

### More interesting explanation

The learner is representing the computation, but its identity is **distributed across symbol + context/role**.

For example, perhaps teacher operation $A$ becomes:

$$
z_3 \text{ in role 1}
$$

but:

$$
z_8 \text{ in role 4}.
$$

Our current analysis says:

> different symbol ⇒ different operation.

A role–filler analysis might instead reveal:

$$
(z_3,R_1)
\sim
(z_8,R_4)
$$

as manifestations of the same underlying filler.

That could explain why:

* whole programs remain excellent;
* composition generalizes;
* depth generalizes;
* but literal motif recurrence collapses.

In other words:

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{semantic recurrence may survive after syntactic recurrence disappears.}
}
$$

That is a major testable hypothesis.

---

# This gives us a cheap diagnostic before changing the architecture

I would not immediately build a TPR learner.

First ask whether our existing trained programs admit a **post-hoc role–filler factorization**.

Take the learned operation at each program position and try to explain it as:

$$
h_{t,j}
\approx
F_{\phi(t,j)}\otimes R_j
$$

or, more generally,

$$
h_{t,j}
\approx
B(F_{k},R_j)
$$

for some learnable binding map $B$.

Then ask:

> Do the hidden “filler” identities become more stable across depth than the raw route symbols?

This is exactly the kind of cheap causal census we like.

# New experiment: RF1 — Does role–filler factorization recover semantic recurrence?

Use existing E6 artifacts.

For each planted teacher primitive occurrence, record:

* raw learner symbol;
* position;
* neighboring symbols;
* activation/operator contribution;
* teacher operation.

Fit a factorization:

$$
x_{t,j}
\approx
B(f_{k},r_j)
$$

without using teacher labels for fitting.

Then evaluate leave-one-context-out.

The primary metric should be:

$$
\boxed{
\text{same-computation filler consistency across roles}
}
$$

versus:

$$
\text{raw-symbol consistency}.
$$

E6 gives us a very strong baseline:

raw motif survival goes roughly:

$$
91\%\to53\%\to13\%\to7\%
$$

as depth rises.

The question is whether filler identity stays high while raw symbols collapse.

If yes, that's a huge result.

# The strongest test would be recombination

Don't stop at clustering.

McCoy's result matters because the components can be recombined systematically, not merely because vectors cluster nicely.

So after fitting:

$$
F_i,\quad R_j,
$$

construct an unseen binding:

$$
B(F_i,R_{j^*})
$$

where filler $i$ never occurred in role $j^*$.

Then substitute it into the executor.

This is basically our E2 H3 test, but at the **factorized representation level**.

We want:

$$
\boxed{
\text{known filler}
+
\text{new role}
\Rightarrow
\text{correct computation}.
}
$$

If that works, we have something much stronger than a post-hoc decomposition.

# It could also give us parameterized primitives

This is where your “it rhymes with lambda calculus” intuition becomes useful.

Rather than twelve unrelated operations:

$$
P_1,\ldots,P_{12},
$$

perhaps some should be represented as:

$$
\boxed{
P(\alpha)
}
$$

where the filler is the function family and the role/argument carries variation.

For example:

$$
\texttt{ADD}\otimes\texttt{ARGUMENT}(3)
$$

rather than creating:

$$
\texttt{ADD1},\texttt{ADD2},\texttt{ADD3},\dots
$$

This connects directly to H39:

$$
\text{shared schema}
+
\text{fast argument}.
$$

McCoy's role–filler framework gives us another mathematical language for the same idea.

---

# I think it also changes how we should think about macros

Right now a macro is:

$$
M=[A,B,C].
$$

But E6.2 showed that trying to squeeze:

$$
A\circ B\circ C
$$

into one same-sized neural slot usually fails.

Maybe the right macro is not a new opaque filler.

Maybe it's a **higher-order structural role**.

For example:

$$
M(x)=
A(B(C(x)))
$$

could be represented as a reusable structural frame:

$$
\boxed{
\texttt{CHAIN}_3
(
A,
B,
C
)
}
$$

Here:

* `CHAIN3` is the role/schema;
* $A,B,C$ are fillers.

That is much closer to a combinator.

Instead of learning a new dense operator, we learn:

$$
\lambda f\,g\,h\,x.\;h(g(f(x))).
$$

Then bind:

$$
f=A,\quad g=B,\quad h=C.
$$

That solves E6.2's capacity problem completely because we no longer require the macro to contain the computation itself.

The macro stores the **structure**.

---

# This suggests a different notion of abstraction

We have mostly been asking whether repeated computation becomes a new primitive:

$$
A\circ B\circ C
\rightarrow
M.
$$

But perhaps the more scalable transformation is:

$$
\boxed{
A\circ B\circ C
\rightarrow
\operatorname{COMPOSE}_3(A,B,C).
}
$$

Then repeated structural motifs can become combinators:

$$
\operatorname{CHAIN}
$$

$$
\operatorname{IF}
$$

$$
\operatorname{MAP}
$$

$$
\operatorname{FOLD}
$$

$$
\operatorname{REPEAT}
$$

and learned operations fill their argument roles.

That is much closer to lambda calculus / combinatory logic.

And it avoids forcing every abstraction into a same-sized neural vector.

---

# This could be the path to loops and branching

This is where I think the McCoy result becomes architecturally powerful.

Instead of treating:

```text
IF
LOOP
MACRO
```

as arbitrary new node types, treat them as **roles / structural combinators**.

For branching:

$$
\operatorname{IF}
(
\underbrace{p}_{\text{predicate role}},
\underbrace{a}_{\text{true role}},
\underbrace{b}_{\text{false role}}
)
$$

For loops:

$$
\operatorname{REPEAT}
(
\underbrace{n}_{\text{count}},
\underbrace{f}_{\text{body}}
)
$$

For map:

$$
\operatorname{MAP}
(
\underbrace{f}_{\text{function}},
\underbrace{x}_{\text{collection}}
).
$$

That is role–filler structure almost literally.

---

# And this makes the interaction-net idea cleaner

Earlier we were debating whether ports were necessary.

Role–filler structure gives a nice answer:

**raw ports probably aren't the learned representation; semantic roles are.**

An `IF` node might have:

$$
\{
\text{condition},
\text{true},
\text{false}
\}
$$

roles.

An interaction-net/compiler layer can internally map these onto ports.

So:

$$
\boxed{
\text{role–filler language}
\rightarrow
\text{typed graph IR}
\rightarrow
\text{local rewrite execution}.
}
$$

The learner reasons in roles and fillers.

The runtime deals with ports/wiring.

That is much cleaner than asking synthesis to manipulate raw ports.

---

# There is another important lesson from McCoy

Your uploaded note already identifies it:

$$
\boxed{
\text{learning mechanism}
\neq
\text{representational structure}.
}
$$

Gradient descent can produce structured representations.

That matters for us because we have occasionally drifted toward:

> If we want programs/symbols/combinators, perhaps we need a discrete symbolic training algorithm.

Not necessarily.

We can continue using continuous differentiable learning for the **formation** of the computational vocabulary, while asking whether the resulting representation factorizes into reusable structural pieces.

So the architecture could be:

$$
\boxed{
\text{continuous neural learning}
\rightarrow
\text{factorized role/filler representation}
\rightarrow
\text{discrete/hierarchical program manipulation}.
}
$$

This matches a lot of our empirical history:

* continuous representation formation often works;
* discrete structure can be difficult early;
* later compilation/discretization can be almost free;
* semantic structure may emerge before explicit symbols.
